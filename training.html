German Shepherd training tips: kind, consistent and effective

German Shepherds learn fast, which is both the joy and the challenge of training one. This guide covers the methods veterinary behaviour experts recommend, the skills worth teaching first, and how to handle the problems Shepherd owners most often meet.

Which training methods work best?

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB), a body of vets and scientists who specialise in behaviour, recommends that only reward-based training methods be used for all dog training and behaviour modification. Its current guidance says aversive methods, including electronic collars, prong collars, choke chains, lead corrections and other physical or psychological punishment, should not be used[1].

That matters for German Shepherds in particular. They are sensitive to their handler, and they are often sold on their guarding instincts. Harsh corrections can make a watchful dog more anxious about the things it is already worried about. In the UK VetCompass study of German Shepherd veterinary records, aggression was one of the five most commonly recorded problems[2]. Fear is a common driver of aggression in dogs, so building confidence rather than suppressing warning signals is the safer path.

What reward-based training looks like

  • Mark and reward. Use a consistent marker, a clicker or a short word such as "yes", at the exact moment your dog does the right thing, then deliver a reward. Food works well for most Shepherds; many also love a quick game of tug as a reward.
  • Set your dog up to succeed. Start in a quiet room, then gradually add distance, duration and distraction one at a time.
  • Prevent rehearsal of unwanted behaviour. Use baby gates, a long line or a crate that your dog is happy in, so mistakes do not become habits while you are teaching the alternative.
  • Keep sessions short. Several five-minute sessions a day beat one long, frustrating session.
  • Teach what you want, not only what you do not want. "Sit to say hello" is easier to train than "don't jump up".

Socialisation: the most important training you will ever do

AVSAB describes the first three months of a puppy's life as the primary and most important period for socialisation, when sociability outweighs fear. During this time puppies should meet as many new people, animals, sights, sounds and environments as can be managed safely and without overwhelming them[3]. It warns that incomplete socialisation increases the risk of fear, avoidance and aggression later in life, and that behaviour problems are the leading cause of dogs being given up to shelters[3].

Because the window starts closing before many puppies have finished their vaccinations, AVSAB's position is that puppies should be socialised before they are fully vaccinated, in safe ways, and that puppy classes can start as early as 7 to 8 weeks of age, provided the puppy has had at least one set of vaccines at least seven days before the first class and has been dewormed[3]. Check the details with your own vet, who knows the disease risks in your area.

A practical socialisation checklist

  • People of different ages, heights and appearances, including people wearing hats, high-visibility jackets, helmets and sunglasses.
  • Calm, vaccinated, dog-friendly adult dogs, plus cats and livestock seen from a distance.
  • Household sounds: the vacuum cleaner, washing machine, doorbell, television and pans.
  • Traffic, bicycles, pushchairs, wheelchairs and joggers, watched from a comfortable distance.
  • Car journeys, the vet's waiting room, a pet shop and a café terrace.
  • Being handled: paws, ears, mouth and tail, paired with treats, to make future vet visits and grooming easier.

Quality matters more than quantity. Let your puppy watch from a distance, reward calm curiosity, and go home if they seem overwhelmed. A puppy that hides, freezes or tries to flee is telling you it is too much. AVSAB advises seeking veterinary guidance for puppies that show fear[3].

The first skills to teach

  1. Name response. Say your dog's name once, reward the moment they look at you. This is the foundation for everything else.
  2. Sit and down. Lure with a treat, then fade the lure into a hand signal and add the word once the behaviour is reliable.
  3. Settle on a mat. Reward your dog for lying calmly on a bed or mat. This is one of the most valuable skills for an energetic breed, because it teaches an "off switch".
  4. Leave it. Teach your dog to turn away from something they want in exchange for something better.
  5. Wait at doors. A short pause before going through doors, gates and car doors is a simple safety habit.
  6. Hand targeting. Teaching your dog to touch their nose to your palm gives you an easy way to move them, get attention back and build confidence.

Lead walking

An adult male Shepherd can weigh 30 to 40 kg[4], so loose-lead walking is worth teaching early, before your puppy is strong enough to pull you over.

  • Use a well-fitted harness or flat collar and a lead of about two metres.
  • Reward your dog often for being by your side, especially in the first few steps of a walk.
  • When the lead goes tight, stop. Wait for slack, or turn and walk the other way, then reward when your dog catches up. Pulling should never get them where they want to go.
  • Build up gradually, from your hallway to the garden, then a quiet street, then a busier one.
  • Allow sniffing breaks on cue ("go sniff") so the walk still meets your dog's needs.

Recall

A reliable recall gives a Shepherd the freedom to run, which makes meeting its exercise needs far easier. Start indoors, then the garden, then a long line of 5 to 10 metres in open spaces, before trusting off-lead recall.

  • Choose a recall word or whistle you will only use for coming back, and always make it worth your dog's while with an extra-good reward.
  • Never call your dog to you for something unpleasant, such as a telling-off or the end of all fun. Call them back, reward and let them go again many times on every walk.
  • Practise "check-ins": reward your dog whenever they choose to look back at you without being asked.
  • Herding and chasing instincts can be strong. Keep your dog on a lead or long line near livestock, wildlife and roads. In the UK, the Countryside Code asks walkers to keep dogs on a lead or in sight and under control around farm animals.

Coping with being alone

Many German Shepherds form strong attachments and like to be close to their people[5]. Teach your dog from the start that being alone is normal and safe.

  • Start with very short absences, a few seconds behind a baby gate, and build up slowly over weeks.
  • Give a long-lasting chew or food toy as you leave, so your departure predicts something good.
  • Keep arrivals and departures low-key.
  • Avoid leaving any dog, especially a young one, alone for long stretches. If you work away from home, plan for a dog walker or day care.

If your dog barks, howls, destroys things or toilets only when left alone, that may be separation-related distress rather than disobedience. Speak to your vet.

Common problem behaviours and what to do

Mouthing and nipping in puppies

All puppies mouth, and Shepherd puppies can be particularly enthusiastic. Redirect onto a toy, end play calmly if teeth touch skin, and make sure your puppy is getting enough sleep: overtired puppies bite more. Never hit or hold a puppy's mouth shut.

Barking at passers-by and visitors

Watchfulness is part of the breed[4]. Thank your dog for the alert, call them away and reward, and reduce triggers by screening windows or keeping your dog out of the front room. Teach visitors to ignore your dog until they settle.

Reactivity on walks

Lunging and barking at other dogs or people is usually caused by fear or frustration. Keep enough distance that your dog can still take treats, reward calm looking at the trigger, and leave if things become too much. A qualified behaviourist can help you build a plan.

Chasing and herding

Some Shepherds try to herd children, cyclists or other pets, sometimes by nipping. Supervise children and dogs together at all times, provide outlets such as chase games with a toy on a flirt pole, and teach a strong "leave it" and recall.

Destructive chewing

Chewing is normal, especially during teething and adolescence. Provide safe chews, rotate toys, puppy-proof the house and make sure exercise and mental work are adequate. See our mental exercise ideas.

When to get professional help

Ask your vet first if your dog's behaviour changes suddenly, or if you see growling, snapping or biting. Pain is a common, often overlooked cause of irritability, and joint problems are common in the breed[2]. Your vet can rule out medical causes and refer you to a qualified behaviourist. In the UK, look for a clinical animal behaviourist or trainer listed with the Animal Behaviour and Training Council (ABTC). In the US, veterinary behaviourists and certified applied animal behaviourists are good options.

Choose any trainer carefully: ask what happens when a dog gets something wrong. If the answer involves pain, fear or intimidation, look elsewhere[1].
