German Shepherd history: from German sheepdog to worldwide working breed

The German Shepherd is a young breed with a well-documented origin. Here is how a cavalry officer's project became one of the most recognisable dogs in the world, and why Britain called it the Alsatian for most of the twentieth century.

Before the breed: Germany's working sheepdogs

Until the late nineteenth century there was no single "German Shepherd". Shepherds across Germany kept herding dogs that were selected for how well they did the job, not for how they looked. Regional styles existed, and the American Kennel Club's history of the breed describes how dogs from Thuringia tended to be smaller and stockier, often with wiry coats and sharp temperaments, while dogs from Württemberg in the south were generally larger, heavier-boned and easier to handle[1].

The 1890s were a period when enthusiasts across Europe were trying to standardise dog breeds[2]. At the same time, changes in farming meant traditional sheepdogs were becoming less common. Max Emil Friedrich von Stephanitz, a cavalry officer who had admired the intelligence and quick responses of the herding dogs he met in the German countryside, set out to preserve and develop them as a formal breed[1].

1899: Horand von Grafrath and the founding of the SV

In April 1899, von Stephanitz and his friend Artur Meyer attended a dog show and saw a four-year-old dog called Hektor Linksrhein. Von Stephanitz bought him on the spot and renamed him Horand von Grafrath[1]. According to the breed's original club, the Verein für Deutsche Schäferhunde (SV), the club was founded on 22 April 1899 in Karlsruhe, with von Stephanitz as its first president, and Horand, a 61 cm male, became number one in its stud book[3]. That stud book has since recorded more than two million German Shepherds[3].

The AKC's account notes that the founding members were a varied group, including shepherds, factory owners, a mayor, an innkeeper, an architect and a magistrate[1]. Horand was not a random find either: he had been deliberately bred, and several of his relatives were also later registered as German Shepherds[1].

Shaping the breed: "working dog breeding"

Von Stephanitz's guiding principle, as the SV describes it, was that "shepherd breeding is working dog breeding"[3]. He bred from herding dogs from southern and central Germany, used Horand heavily at stud, and relied on planned line-breeding in the early years to fix the traits he wanted[3]. The AKC notes that through grandsons such as Heinz von Starkenburg, Beowulf and Pilot, Horand appears in the pedigrees of virtually every German Shepherd alive today[1].

Two ideas from that period still shape the breed:

  • Function first. Von Stephanitz disagreed with people who wanted the breed to be only a show dog[1]. The modern FCI standard echoes this by warning that exaggerated hindquarter angulation reduces stamina and working ability[4].
  • Temperament matters as much as looks. The AKC notes that German Shepherds at shows are still given a brief temperament check by the judge to confirm they are stable[1].

As traditional herding work declined, von Stephanitz promoted the breed for police and military service. The Royal Kennel Club summarises the breed's development as a herding dog first, then a working dog for the police and armed forces[5].

The world wars

The First World War made the German Shepherd famous and cost it dearly. The SV records that about 7,000 German Shepherds were killed while serving as telephone-cable layers, messengers and medical dogs, and that the breed's genetic variety was significantly reduced as a result[3]. The Royal Kennel Club notes that from the First World War onward, the breed's bravery and temperament earned it worldwide recognition[5].

After the war, membership of the SV soared. The club reported 57,000 members at the start of the 1920s[3]. Soldiers returning from the front helped spread interest abroad, and the AKC highlights two film-star Shepherds of the period, Rin Tin Tin and Strongheart, whose fame drove a surge in popularity[1].

The 1930s were a darker chapter. The SV itself acknowledges that after 1933, independent associations were absorbed into a Nazi-era national body and the breed "was abused for propaganda purposes", and that thousands of Shepherds were used and killed in the Second World War[3]. Von Stephanitz was forced out of the club and died in 1936[1]. After the war, the SV split between East and West Germany, and was reunited only in 1990[3].

The Alsatian years in Britain

Anti-German feeling after the First World War led to a change of name in Britain. The breed was registered as the Alsatian Wolf Dog, and campaigns by breeders who worried that "wolf dog" would hurt the breed's reputation eventually got that part dropped[2]. The name Alsatian lasted for around five decades. In 1977, campaigns by enthusiasts persuaded the British kennel clubs to register the breed as the German Shepherd Dog again, although "Alsatian" remained in brackets in the Kennel Club's formal breed name until 2010[2].

This is why many people in the UK, particularly older generations, still call the breed an Alsatian. It is the same dog.

In the United States

The American Kennel Club began registering German Shepherd Dogs in 1908[6]. During the First World War, the AKC asked the breed's national club to remove "German" from both its own name and the breed name. The club became the Shepherd Club of America for a time[1]. The breed's popularity recovered, and in 1930 von Stephanitz travelled to the US to judge at the Morris & Essex Kennel Club show[1]. Today the AKC ranks the German Shepherd among the most popular breeds in the country[7].

The breed today

The German Shepherd now works in an unusually wide range of roles. The Royal Kennel Club lists guide work for blind people, tracking and obedience alongside police and military service[5]. The FCI standard describes a dog suitable as a "companion, guard, protection, service and herding dog"[4]. The SV describes itself as the largest single-breed club in the world, with more than 50,000 members, and the World Union of German Shepherd Associations reports more than 500,000 members worldwide[3].

Not everything about the modern breed is positive. The Royal Kennel Club acknowledges a "marked division of breed type" and fierce debate over how the breed's appearance has changed in the last fifty years[5]. In the UK, one large study of veterinary records found that German Shepherds made up a shrinking share of each year's puppies, falling from 3.5 per cent of dogs born in 2005 to 2.2 per cent of those born in 2013[8]. The health issues behind some of that debate are covered on our health page.

Timeline

  1. 1890s: Efforts to standardise German herding dogs[2].
  2. April 1899: Von Stephanitz buys Horand von Grafrath; the SV is founded on 22 April with Horand as stud book number one[3].
  3. 1908: The AKC first registers the breed[6].
  4. 1914 to 1918: Around 7,000 Shepherds killed in wartime service[3]; the breed is renamed in Britain and the US[1].
  5. Early 1920s: SV membership reaches 57,000[3].
  6. 1936: Death of Max von Stephanitz[1].
  7. 1977: The breed is registered as the German Shepherd Dog in Britain again[2].
  8. 1990: East and West German SV memberships reunite[3].
  9. 2010: "(Alsatian)" removed from the formal UK breed name[2].
