German Shepherd breed overview: size, coat, colours and temperament

What a German Shepherd actually is, physically and mentally, according to the people who write the breed standards, and what that means for a pet owner.

Size and weight

The German Shepherd is a large dog by most people's standards, although the official German standard calls it "medium-size". The standard maintained by the Verein für Deutsche Schäferhunde (SV), the breed's original club, and published by the international federation FCI, sets these limits[1]:

Height and weight in the main breed standards
FCI / SV standard (metric)American Kennel Club
Male height60 to 65 cm24 to 26 in
Female height55 to 60 cm22 to 24 in
Male weight30 to 40 kg65 to 90 lb
Female weight22 to 32 kg50 to 70 lb

Sources: FCI standard No. 166[1]; AKC breed page[2].

The AKC's upper weight figure for males, 90 lb, is about 41 kg, a little above the FCI's 40 kg. In practice, many pet Shepherds fall comfortably inside both ranges. Real-world UK data give a useful reality check: a study of veterinary records from practices across the UK found a median bodyweight of 36.0 kg for male German Shepherds across all ages, and showed that puppies grow rapidly through their first year before males level off at a higher adult weight than females[3].

Weight matters for more than the lead. In the same study, overweight or obesity was one of the five most commonly recorded problems in the breed[3], and extra weight puts more load on joints that are already prone to dysplasia. A lean Shepherd should have a visible waist when viewed from above and ribs you can feel easily under a thin layer of fat. Our feeding guide covers how to keep it that way.

Build and movement

The FCI standard describes the breed as "slightly elongated, powerful and well-muscled", with a body length that exceeds the height at the withers by about 10 to 17 per cent. The head is wedge-shaped, the ears stand erect, and the tail hangs in a gentle curve at rest[1].

Above all, the standard calls the German Shepherd "a trotter": a dog built to cover ground at a steady, efficient pace for hours, as it would have done when patrolling the edge of a flock. It explicitly warns that over-angulation of the hindquarters "reduces the stability and the stamina, and thereby the working ability"[1]. That sentence sits at the heart of a long-running argument about the breed's shape, covered below.

Coat types: short (stock) and long

All German Shepherds have a double coat: a dense, harsh outer layer of guard hair over a soft undercoat. The standard recognises two varieties[1]:

  • Double coat (often called stock coat or short coat): close-fitting, dense guard hair, short on the head and legs, slightly longer on the neck, with moderate "trousers" on the back of the thighs.
  • Long double coat: longer, softer guard hair that does not lie flat, with tufts on the ears, feathering on the legs and a bushier tail, still with an undercoat underneath.

The Royal Kennel Club notes that the long-haired variety was once frowned upon in Germany but has now been officially recognised in the breed standard[4]. Both coat types shed. The undercoat is what ends up drifting across your floor, especially during seasonal moults. Grooming advice is on the care page.

Colours

Most people picture the classic black-and-tan Shepherd with a dark "saddle" across its back, but the breed comes in a wider range. The FCI standard allows black with reddish-brown, brown or yellow to light grey markings, solid black, and grey with darker shading (often called sable), and states that the colour white is not allowed[1].

The Royal Kennel Club lists bi-colour, black, black and gold, black and tan, dark sable, gold sable, grey, grey sable and sable as breed-standard colours. It classes white, blue, liver and Isabella (a pale, washed-out shade) as non-breed-standard colours, and adds a sensible reminder that "health and temperament should always be a priority over colour"[4].

If a seller charges extra for a "rare" colour such as blue, liver or white, treat that as a warning sign rather than a selling point. Colour has nothing to do with whether a dog will be healthy or good-natured.

Temperament

The FCI standard describes the ideal character as "well-balanced (with strong nerves)", "self-assured", "good-natured" except when provoked, and "attentive and willing to please", with the resilience to work as a "companion, guard, protection, service and herding dog"[1]. The Royal Kennel Club sums the breed up as a versatile worker noted for bravery and intelligence[4]. The AKC notes that Shepherds tend to be reserved with strangers but friendly with people they have already met[5].

In everyday terms, that usually translates into:

  • A strong bond with their own people. Many Shepherds like to stay close to their family and follow them from room to room. Some struggle with being left alone, and they need to be taught gradually that it is fine.
  • Natural watchfulness. They notice everything and are inclined to alert you to visitors. This is useful in moderation but needs managing so it does not tip into constant barking or guarding.
  • A need for mental work. A Shepherd that is walked but never trained or given problems to solve will often invent its own jobs, such as chewing, digging, fence-running or herding the children.
  • High trainability. This is the upside of all that brainpower. Shepherds learn quickly, and that includes learning bad habits.

Temperament is not guaranteed by breed. The UK veterinary study found aggression among the five most commonly recorded issues in German Shepherds attending primary-care practices[3]. That does not mean Shepherds are inherently aggressive. It does show that genetics, early socialisation and training all matter, which is why we put so much emphasis on choosing a breeder carefully and on socialisation.

Working lines and show lines

You will often hear breeders and owners talk about "working line" and "show line" Shepherds, and sometimes about German versus American or British show types. The Royal Kennel Club acknowledges this openly: it says the evolution of the breed and its changed appearance over the past fifty years "has provoked fierce debate", and that the breed now has "a marked division of breed type"[4].

Broadly, and with plenty of overlap between individual dogs:

  • Working lines are bred mainly for drive and ability in sports such as tracking, obedience and protection work. They are often very energetic and intense, which can be a lot for a first-time owner.
  • Show lines are bred mainly to meet the breed standard in the show ring. Some show types have been criticised for a sharply sloping topline and very angulated hindquarters, which is the concern behind the FCI standard's warning against over-angulation[1].

Rather than choosing a "type" by label, look at the actual parents. Can they move freely and soundly? Are they calm and confident with visitors? Have they been health-tested? Those questions matter far more than which side of the debate a breeder is on.

Who the breed suits, and who it does not

A German Shepherd is likely to suit you if you:

  • enjoy daily outdoor activity and can realistically give a healthy adult well over an hour, and often closer to two, of exercise and activity every day[4];
  • like the idea of training as an ongoing hobby rather than a short course;
  • are home for much of the day, or can arrange a dog walker or day care;
  • can live with shedding and the occasional muddy, 35 kg dog on the sofa.

It may be the wrong choice if you want a low-maintenance dog, live in a small flat with no easy access to open space, are out at work all day, or are not comfortable handling a strong dog. In those situations a different breed, or an older rescue Shepherd whose temperament and energy level are already known, may be a better fit.
