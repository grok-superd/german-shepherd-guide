German Shepherd health problems and lifespan

The conditions that most often affect German Shepherds, what the research says about how long they live, the health tests to look for in a breeder, and the warning signs that mean a call to your vet.

Not veterinary advice. This page is general information for owners. It cannot diagnose or treat your dog. If you are worried about your dog's health, contact your vet, and in an emergency call your vet or nearest out-of-hours clinic straight away.

How long do German Shepherds live?

The answer depends on where the figure comes from:

  • The American Kennel Club gives a life expectancy of 12 to 14 years[1].
  • The Royal Kennel Club lists the breed's lifespan as over 10 years[2].
  • A study of UK primary-care veterinary records, run by the Royal Veterinary College's VetCompass programme, found a median longevity of 10.3 years, with half of the dogs dying between 8.0 and 12.1 years of age[3].

The VetCompass figure is probably the most realistic guide for a pet owner, because it is based on the actual age at death of German Shepherds seen by hundreds of ordinary vet practices, rather than an estimate for typical healthy dogs. In that study, females lived longer than males, with a median of 11.1 years compared with 9.7 years[3]. The authors also note that the median for dogs of all breeds measured in a similar way was 12.0 years, and that larger breeds generally live shorter lives, so German Shepherds are "not particularly short-lived given their large body-size"[3].

The same study found that the most common causes of death were musculoskeletal disorders (16.3 per cent of deaths with a recorded cause), inability to stand (14.9 per cent), cancer (14.5 per cent) and spinal cord disorders (13.6 per cent)[3]. In other words, problems with the back legs, joints and spine are among the main reasons German Shepherds die or are put to sleep. Many of the steps on this page are about protecting mobility for as long as possible.

What UK vet records show: the most common problems

The VetCompass researchers reviewed the 2013 veterinary records of a random sample of German Shepherds. The five most commonly recorded disorders were[3]:

DisorderShare of dogs affected
Ear inflammation (otitis externa)7.9%
Osteoarthritis5.5%
Diarrhoea5.2%
Overweight or obesity5.2%

Source: O'Neill et al., 2017[3].

These are everyday problems rather than rare genetic diseases, and several are partly within an owner's control. Weekly ear checks, keeping your dog lean and careful socialisation all address items on this list. Our care guide and training guide cover the practical side.

Hip and elbow dysplasia

Hip dysplasia is a deformity of the hip that develops during growth. In a healthy hip, the ball at the top of the thigh bone and the socket in the pelvis grow at matching rates. In hip dysplasia they do not, so the joint becomes loose, which leads over time to arthritis[4]. The VCA describes it as a genetic disease that is also affected by diet, environment, exercise, growth rate, muscle mass and hormones, and recommends keeping large-breed puppies at a normal, lean weight rather than overfeeding them or encouraging them to grow big[4].

Two points often surprise owners. First, X-ray changes and symptoms do not always match: some dogs with marked changes show no signs, while others with mild changes are in significant pain[4]. Second, hip dysplasia is a condition of growth, so what happens in puppyhood matters.

Signs to look for include stiffness after rest, a "bunny-hopping" gait with both back legs moving together, reluctance to climb stairs or jump, a swaying walk, and loss of muscle over the hindquarters. Elbow dysplasia, a related developmental problem of the front legs, often shows as front-leg lameness in young, growing dogs.

What owners can do: buy from a breeder who hip- and elbow-scores breeding dogs, feed a large-breed puppy food at the recommended amount, avoid over-exercising young puppies, and keep adult dogs lean. If your dog is diagnosed, your vet may suggest weight management, controlled exercise, physiotherapy or hydrotherapy, pain relief or, in some cases, surgery.

Understanding hip scores

In the UK, hips are assessed under the BVA/Kennel Club Hip Dysplasia Scheme. Each hip is scored from 0 to 53, giving a total from 0 to 106, and lower is better. The BVA advises comparing a dog's score with the breed median and selecting breeding dogs at or, ideally, below it[5]. In the US, the Orthopedic Foundation for Animals (OFA) grades hips and elbows differently, but the principle is the same: ask to see the results for both parents.

Degenerative myelopathy (DM)

Degenerative myelopathy is a disease of the spinal cord that causes slowly progressive weakness and eventually paralysis of the back legs. It is sometimes called German Shepherd degenerative myelopathy because of its association with the breed, and has been compared with some forms of ALS (motor neurone disease) in people[6]. A mutation in a gene called SOD1 increases the risk, although not every dog carrying two copies develops the disease, so other factors are thought to be involved[6].

DM usually starts in older dogs with wobbliness, scuffing of the back toes or crossing of the back legs. It is not painful in itself, which helps distinguish it from arthritis, although the two can occur together. There is no cure, but physiotherapy and keeping a dog active may help maintain mobility for longer. A DNA test is available, and the Royal Kennel Club recommends it for breeding German Shepherds[2]. The VCA advises that at-risk breeds should be tested before breeding[6].

Bloat (gastric dilatation and volvulus)

GDV is a life-threatening emergency in which the stomach fills with gas and can twist on itself. It is most common in large, deep-chested dogs[7]. Even with treatment, the VCA reports a mortality rate of 10 to 45 per cent in relatively uncomplicated cases, and says immediate veterinary attention, within minutes to a few hours, is needed[7].

Signs include restlessness or distress, heavy drooling, retching without producing anything, a swollen belly and difficulty breathing, progressing to collapse[7]. Risk factors and prevention steps, including meal size and the option of a preventive gastropexy, are covered on our care page.

Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI)

EPI occurs when the pancreas does not produce enough digestive enzymes, so food is not properly digested. The VCA notes that it is seen more commonly in German Shepherds than in many other breeds and is suspected to be inherited in the breed, mainly through a progressive loss of pancreatic cells[8]. Typical signs are weight loss despite a good or increased appetite, and large volumes of pale, greasy or loose faeces. EPI is diagnosed with a blood test and is usually managed long-term with enzyme supplements added to food, so it is worth raising with your vet if your dog is losing weight while eating well.

Ears, skin and digestion

Ear inflammation was the most commonly recorded problem in the UK study and diarrhoea the third[3]. Neither sounds dramatic, but both cause real discomfort and can become chronic. Shepherds can also be prone to skin problems, including allergies. Practical steps include checking ears weekly, keeping the coat well groomed so the skin can breathe, changing diets gradually, and seeing your vet early for repeated ear infections, itching or loose stools rather than trying home remedies for weeks.

Health tests to look for before breeding

If you are buying a puppy, ask the breeder for health test results for both parents. The Royal Kennel Club recommends the following for German Shepherds[2]:

  • Hip scoring under the BVA/KC Hip Dysplasia Scheme (strongly recommended as a minimum).
  • Elbow grading under the BVA/KC Elbow Dysplasia Scheme (strongly recommended as a minimum).
  • DNA test for degenerative myelopathy (recommended best practice).
  • Eye testing under the BVA/KC/ISDS Eye Scheme (recommended best practice).

You can check UK results yourself using the Royal Kennel Club's Health Test Results Finder, which also shows inbreeding coefficients for a planned mating[2]. US buyers can search the OFA database. A breeder who is reluctant to show you results, or who says their dogs "don't need testing because they're healthy", is not one to buy from. Read more in our guide to choosing a breeder.

Signs to take seriously

Contact a vet immediately if your dog shows any signs of bloat (retching without vomiting, a swollen belly, restlessness, drooling, collapse)[7], sudden inability to use the back legs, difficulty breathing, or severe pain.

Book a vet appointment soon if you notice:

  • limping, stiffness or reluctance to exercise that lasts more than a day or two;
  • scuffing or dragging of the back feet, or a wobbly back end;
  • weight loss, especially with a normal or increased appetite;
  • persistent diarrhoea or greasy, bulky stools;
  • head shaking, ear scratching or smelly ears;
  • new lumps, or any sudden change in behaviour such as irritability or growling.

Regular check-ups, at least once a year and more often for older dogs, give your vet the chance to spot weight changes, joint problems and lumps early. Pet insurance taken out when your dog is young and healthy can make it easier to say yes to treatment later.
