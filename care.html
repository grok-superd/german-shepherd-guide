German Shepherd care: exercise, grooming and feeding

Practical, day-to-day care for an adult German Shepherd: how much exercise is enough, how to cope with the shedding, how to feed for a lean, healthy weight, and the routine checks that catch problems early.

How much exercise does a German Shepherd need?

The Royal Kennel Club puts the German Shepherd in its highest exercise category: more than two hours a day for a healthy adult[1]. That figure surprises many new owners, but it reflects what the breed was built for. The FCI standard describes the Shepherd as a trotter designed for stamina[2], a dog that would once have spent the whole day on the move around a flock.

Two hours does not have to mean two hours of marching. A realistic day for a fit adult might combine:

  • one longer walk with time off the lead in a safe, enclosed or quiet area, if recall is reliable;
  • one or two shorter walks for sniffing and toilet breaks;
  • a training session or game built into a walk, such as searching for a hidden toy, recall practice or a few minutes of heelwork.

Adjust for the individual. Working-line dogs often need more, while older dogs and those with arthritis need shorter, gentler outings spread through the day. Joint problems are common in the breed: osteoarthritis was the second most commonly recorded disorder in German Shepherds in a large UK study of veterinary records[3]. If your dog is stiff after rest, slows down on walks, or is reluctant to jump into the car, ask your vet before increasing exercise.

Exercise for puppies and young dogs

Puppies have different needs. Their joints are still developing, and hip dysplasia is a condition of growth that is influenced by exercise, diet and growth rate as well as genetics[4]. Short, frequent play sessions on soft ground are better than long road walks or repeated jumping. Our puppy page goes into more detail.

Mental exercise matters as much as walks

A Shepherd that gets plenty of walks but no mental stimulation can still be restless. The breed was selected to think and respond to a handler[5], and many behaviour problems in young Shepherds are really boredom problems. Good ways to tire the brain include:

  • Scent work. Hide treats or a favourite toy around the house or garden and let your dog search. Gradually make the hides harder.
  • Food puzzles. Feed part of the daily ration from a puzzle toy, a scatter feed in the grass or a frozen stuffed toy.
  • Short training sessions. Five minutes of teaching something new, such as a trick, a "settle" on a mat or a retrieve, is surprisingly tiring.
  • Dog sports. Tracking, obedience, rally, agility (for adults with sound joints) and herding clubs all suit the breed. Many breed and training clubs in the UK and US run beginner classes.

See our training guide for reward-based methods to use in these sessions.

Grooming and shedding

The Royal Kennel Club recommends grooming a German Shepherd more than once a week, and lists the breed as one that sheds[1]. Both coat varieties, the shorter stock coat and the long coat, have a dense undercoat[2]. It is the undercoat that comes out in clumps, especially during seasonal moults, when many owners find they are brushing out enough hair to fill a carrier bag.

A simple grooming routine

  1. Two or three times a week, brush the whole coat with a slicker brush or pin brush, working in the direction of growth and paying attention to the thick fur on the neck, chest and "trousers" on the back legs.
  2. During a moult, brush daily and use an undercoat rake to lift loose fur from close to the skin. Be gentle: dragging hard with a de-shedding blade can irritate the skin.
  3. Long-coated dogs need extra attention behind the ears, in the armpits and around the tail, where mats form most easily.
  4. Bathe only when needed, using a dog shampoo, and rinse very thoroughly because residue trapped in the undercoat can irritate the skin. Dry the coat fully, down to the skin.

Do not shave a German Shepherd to reduce shedding unless your vet advises it for a medical reason. The double coat helps protect the skin, and clipping it short does not stop the dog shedding.

Grooming sessions are also a good time for a quick health check: run your hands over your dog to feel for lumps, sore spots, ticks or grass seeds, and look at the skin for redness or flaking.

Ears, teeth and nails

Ears. Ear inflammation (otitis externa) was the single most commonly recorded disorder in German Shepherds in the UK VetCompass study, recorded in about 7.9 per cent of the German Shepherds whose 2013 records were reviewed[3]. Large, upright ears are not immune to problems. Look inside your dog's ears weekly. Healthy ears are pale pink, clean and do not smell. Head shaking, scratching, redness, discharge or a yeasty smell all mean a trip to the vet. Do not push cotton buds into the ear canal.

Teeth. Daily tooth brushing with a dog toothpaste is the most effective home dental care. Start gently, letting your dog lick the paste first, and build up over a week or two.

Nails. Active dogs that walk on pavements may wear their nails down naturally, but many need trimming every few weeks. If you can hear nails clicking on a hard floor, they are probably too long. Ask your vet or a groomer to show you how to trim safely if you are unsure.

Feeding an adult German Shepherd

There is no single right amount of food for a German Shepherd. Needs vary with age, size, activity level and neutering status. The PDSA recommends using the guidelines on the food packet as a starting point, then adjusting to keep your dog at a healthy weight[6]. Weighing food on kitchen scales is more accurate than using a scoop[7].

Keeping weight in check is one of the most useful things you can do for a Shepherd. Overweight or obesity was among the five most commonly recorded problems in the breed in the UK study[3], and extra weight adds strain to hips and elbows that may already be affected by dysplasia[4]. Useful habits:

  • Choose a complete food. A food labelled "complete" is designed to provide everything a dog needs without supplements. Feed one designed for your dog's life stage.
  • Count the treats. PDSA advises keeping treats to no more than 10 per cent of the daily food allowance, and reducing meals to compensate[7]. Using part of the daily kibble as training rewards is an easy win.
  • Change foods slowly. PDSA recommends introducing a new food gradually over a couple of weeks to avoid stomach upsets[6]. Diarrhoea was one of the most common reasons German Shepherds saw a vet in the UK study[3], so a sensitive stomach is worth guarding against.
  • Check body condition every month. You should be able to feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat, see a waist from above, and see the belly tuck up from the side.
  • Weigh your dog regularly. Many vet practices let you use their scales for free. Write the result down so you can spot slow changes.

Puppies need a different approach. They should stay on a large-breed puppy food for longer than small breeds: PDSA's vets suggest around 18 months for large and giant breeds[8]. See our German Shepherd puppy feeding guide.

Mealtimes and bloat risk

Gastric dilatation and volvulus (GDV), often called bloat, is a life-threatening emergency in which the stomach fills with gas and can twist. It is seen most often in large, deep-chested dogs[9], and the German Shepherd's deep chest puts it in that body type[2]. The VCA lists several factors linked to higher risk, including feeding only one meal a day, eating fast, being underweight, a nervous temperament, a family history of bloat, and being middle-aged or older. It also notes that exercise after a large meal or a large drink may increase risk[9].

Sensible precautions based on that list:

  • split the daily ration into two or more meals rather than one;
  • slow down fast eaters with a slow-feeder bowl or a scatter feed;
  • avoid vigorous exercise straight after a big meal or a big drink;
  • ask your vet whether a preventive gastropexy (a procedure that attaches the stomach to the body wall) makes sense for your dog. The VCA describes this as the most effective means of prevention[9].
Emergency signs. Restlessness, drooling, retching without bringing anything up, a swollen belly, laboured breathing or collapse can all signal bloat[9]. Call your vet or nearest emergency clinic immediately. Do not wait to see if it passes.

A sample daily routine for an adult Shepherd

An example only. Adapt it to your dog's age, health and your own schedule.
TimeActivity
MorningToilet break, then a 45 to 60 minute walk with sniffing time and some recall or heelwork practice. Breakfast after the walk, part of it from a puzzle feeder.
MiddayShort toilet walk. Quiet rest. If you are out, a dog walker or a long-lasting chew.
AfternoonTen minutes of training or a scent game in the garden or house.
EveningA second, longer outing or a play session. Dinner, followed by a calm hour rather than boisterous play.
WeeklyTwo or three full brushes, an ear check, nail check, a weigh-in or body condition check.

A well-exercised, mentally busy German Shepherd is usually a calm one at home. If yours is not settling despite plenty of activity, it may be worth talking to your vet and a qualified behaviourist. Our training page covers common problem behaviours.
