German Shepherd puppies: choosing, feeding and the first months

Getting a German Shepherd puppy right starts long before you bring one home. This guide covers finding a responsible breeder or rescue, the questions to ask, feeding a large-breed puppy, safe exercise and the first weeks at home.

Choosing a breeder

A German Shepherd's adult health and temperament are shaped partly by genetics, so the choice of breeder is one of the biggest decisions you will make. The Royal Kennel Club recommends that breeders hip-score and elbow-grade breeding dogs as a minimum, and also DNA test for degenerative myelopathy and eye test as best practice[1]. Hip dysplasia in particular is a genetic disease[2], so tested parents reduce, though they cannot eliminate, the risk.

Signs of a responsible breeder include:

  • You can meet the mother with her puppies, in the home or kennel where they were raised.
  • Health test certificates for both parents are shown willingly, and the results can be checked independently[1].
  • The parents are calm, confident and friendly with visitors.
  • The breeder asks you plenty of questions about your home, work pattern and experience.
  • Puppies are at least eight weeks old before going home, and have been handled and introduced to normal household sights and sounds.
  • The breeder provides a written contract, information on feeding, worming and vaccinations, and offers to take the dog back at any age if you cannot keep it.

Be wary of sellers who offer to meet you in a car park, always have puppies available, cannot show the mother, push "rare" colours such as blue, liver or white (which are not breed-standard colours[1]), or ask for a deposit before you have seen the puppies. These are common signs of puppy farming. In England, government guidance explains that Lucy's Law bans commercial third-party sales of puppies, so you should buy directly from the breeder or a rehoming centre, and licensed breeders must show puppies with their mother.

Questions to ask a breeder

  1. Can I see the hip and elbow scores for both parents, and how do they compare with the breed median?[3]
  2. Have the parents been DNA tested for degenerative myelopathy, and what were the results?[1]
  3. Have the parents had an eye test under the BVA/KC/ISDS scheme?[1]
  4. Are the parents from working or show lines, and what are their temperaments like day to day?[1]
  5. How have the puppies been socialised so far?
  6. What food are they eating, how much and how often? The PDSA suggests asking exactly this[4].
  7. Have the puppies been wormed, vaccinated and microchipped? (In the UK, all dogs must be microchipped and registered by the time they are 8 weeks old.)
  8. What support do you offer after the sale, and will you take the dog back if needed?

Adopting a rescue German Shepherd

Not every Shepherd needs to start as a puppy. Breed-specific rescues and general rehoming charities in both the UK and US regularly have German Shepherds looking for homes, including adults whose temperament, energy and health are already known. A good rescue will assess the dog, be honest about any issues, and match it to your household. Adult rescue dogs can be an excellent choice for people who would find the puppy stage difficult, although they may need time and patience to settle.

Preparing your home

  • A crate or puppy pen large enough for an adult Shepherd, introduced as a comfortable den, never as punishment.
  • Baby gates to manage access to stairs and rooms.
  • Chew toys of different textures, and puppy-proofing: cables, shoes, plants and children's toys out of reach.
  • The same food the breeder uses, so you do not change diet on top of everything else.
  • A secure garden with no gaps under fences or gates.
  • A vet appointment booked for the first week, and pet insurance in place from day one.

German Shepherd puppy feeding

Large-breed puppies need careful feeding. The goal is steady growth, not maximum growth. The VCA advises keeping large-breed puppies at a normal, lean weight during growth rather than overfeeding them or encouraging them to grow big, because growth rate is one of the factors that affects hip dysplasia[2].

What to feed

  • A complete puppy food designed for large breeds. PDSA explains that adult food does not have the right balance of nutrients for growth, and that larger breeds need puppy food with the right balance to support bone and joint development[4].
  • No extra calcium or supplements unless your vet advises them. A complete diet already contains the minerals a puppy needs, and the Purina Institute notes that excess calcium can be harmful to growing puppies[5].
  • Stay on puppy food for long enough. PDSA's vets suggest that large and giant breeds usually need puppy food until around 18 months[6]. Some sources suggest growth diets for up to 24 months in giant breeds[5]; German Shepherds are usually somewhere in between, so ask your vet when to switch.

How much and how often

  • Use the feeding guide on the pack, based on your puppy's age and expected adult weight, and weigh portions with digital scales rather than a cup[4].
  • Split the daily amount into several small meals. The Purina Institute suggests up to four meals a day until about six months of age, then two meals a day[5].
  • Measured meals are better than leaving food down all day, because portion control helps avoid excessively fast growth[5].
  • Count training treats as part of the daily allowance, keeping them to about 10 per cent[4].
  • Weigh your puppy regularly and adjust the amount as they grow[7]. German Shepherd puppies grow quickly through their first year[8], so portions change often.

If you want to change foods, PDSA recommends doing it slowly over a couple of weeks once your puppy has settled in[7].

Exercise for growing puppies

Puppies need play and exploration every day, but not long, repetitive exercise. Good options are short walks on varied surfaces, free play in the garden, gentle training games and supervised play with calm adult dogs. Avoid long runs, jogging alongside a bike, repeated jumping, endless ball-throwing and lots of stairs while your puppy is growing.

You may see a "five minutes of formal exercise per month of age, up to twice a day" rule quoted online. It is a common rule of thumb rather than a research-based limit, but its spirit, building up gradually and letting the puppy choose to stop, is sensible. Ask your vet for advice for your individual puppy, particularly if there is any family history of hip or elbow problems.

The first weeks at home

  • Toilet training: take your puppy outside after waking, eating, playing and every hour or two in between, and reward calmly when they go in the right place.
  • Sleep: young puppies need a lot of sleep. Overtired puppies are bitey and frantic, so plan quiet rest periods in a crate or pen.
  • Socialisation: AVSAB considers the first three months the most important period for socialisation[9]. Start gently from the first week with new sounds, people and places, carried if necessary before vaccinations are complete. See our socialisation checklist.
  • Puppy classes: AVSAB says classes can start from 7 to 8 weeks, once your puppy has had at least one set of vaccines at least seven days earlier and has been dewormed[9]. Choose a class that uses reward-based methods[10].
  • Handling: touch paws, ears and mouth every day, with treats, so grooming and vet visits stay easy.
  • Alone time: start teaching short, positive periods alone straight away. See coping with being alone.

The puppy months are hard work, but they set the pattern for the next decade or more. For the adult years, see our care guide and health guide.
