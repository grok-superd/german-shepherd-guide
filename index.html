Is a German Shepherd right for you?

German Shepherds are large, athletic, intelligent dogs that were bred to work alongside people all day. That heritage is the key to understanding them. The Royal Kennel Club describes the breed as a "versatile worker and service dog noted for bravery and intelligence", and lists its exercise needs as more than two hours a day, with grooming more than once a week and a coat that sheds[1]. In the United States, the American Kennel Club places the breed in its Herding Group and ranks it among the country's most popular breeds[2].

Popularity can be misleading, though. A dog that thrives in a busy, active household with time for training can become frustrated, noisy or destructive in a home where it is left alone for long stretches with nothing to do. Before anything else, it is worth being honest about four things:

  • Time. Daily exercise is only part of it. Shepherds also need mental work: training sessions, scent games, puzzle feeders and a job to do.
  • Space and strength. The FCI breed standard sets a height of 60 to 65 cm (about 24 to 26 in) at the shoulder for males and a weight of 30 to 40 kg[3]. A strong, untrained adult on a lead is hard work for anyone.
  • Hair. The double coat sheds all year and heavily at certain times. If you dislike dog hair on clothes and furniture, this breed will test you.
  • Health costs. Like most large breeds, Shepherds are prone to joint problems and some inherited conditions. Insurance, or savings set aside for vet bills, is sensible.

None of this is meant to put you off. Owners who match the breed's needs often describe a loyal, eager, endlessly trainable companion. The aim of this site is simply to give you accurate expectations, with every key fact linked to its source so you can check it yourself.

Why do the lifespan figures differ? The AKC gives a typical range for the breed, while the UK figure comes from a study of real veterinary records from hundreds of practices, so it includes dogs that died young from illness or accidents. Our health page explains this in more detail.

Where to start

If you are still deciding whether to get a German Shepherd, read the breed overview and the health guide first, because temperament and inherited health problems are the two areas where a good breeder makes the biggest difference. If you already have a puppy on the way, go straight to puppies and then training: the first few months of socialisation have a lasting effect on adult behaviour. If you live with an adult Shepherd, the care page covers exercise, grooming and feeding in practical detail.

Many common questions, such as whether Shepherds are good with children, how long they live, or how much they shed, have short answers on the FAQ page.

How this guide is written

Every factual claim on this site is linked to a source we consider reliable: the kennel clubs that maintain the breed standards, veterinary organisations, and peer-reviewed research. Where sources disagree, we say so rather than picking whichever number sounds best. We do not publish made-up statistics, invented expert quotes or product reviews we have not done. You can read more about our approach on the about page.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. Your own vet knows your dog and should always be your first call about health.
